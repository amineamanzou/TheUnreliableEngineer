I sometimes finish reading a three-page senior profile knowing twenty-five technologies.

I still do not know which mission fits the person.

The document is accurate. Kubernetes is there. So are cloud, DevOps, SRE, platform, security and architecture. Every experience contains a list of serious responsibilities.

The difficulty appears when the career needs to match a client situation.

Can this person recover an unstable production platform? Build an internal platform? Reduce observability cost? Help a team adopt SRE practices? Lead a migration? Restore control over a delivery process that keeps drifting?

The profile makes the reader reconstruct the answer alone.

The four phrases below are composite examples. They reproduce structures I encounter regularly without quoting an individual résumé or turning a client case into content.

## 1. “Senior DevOps / Cloud / SRE / Platform / DevSecOps”

This line tries to cover every search.

It mainly creates five different interpretations.

A recruiter may associate `DevOps` with CI/CD automation. A platform team sees an internal product for developers. An SRE manager expects SLOs, incident management and capacity planning. A cloud client thinks about migration or landing zones. Security adds another set of expectations.

The person may genuinely have worked across all of them.

The title still needs to provide an entry point.

I prefer wording that links the role to a context:

> Senior SRE / Platform engineer — production platform recovery, OpenTelemetry standardization and safer deployments.

The remaining terms can stay in skills and experience. The top of the profile should orient the reading, not win a lexical coverage contest.

Exact keywords still help search visibility. They work better after the person has selected the problem they want to be called to solve.

## 2. “Passionate about technology and continuous improvement”

The sentence is probably sincere.

It uses the most expensive space in the profile without helping anyone decide.

Passion provides no context, autonomy level or type of constraint. Continuous improvement may describe a retrospective, a complete migration or renaming three Jenkins jobs.

A senior profile benefits from replacing intent with observable behavior:

> I take over delivery chains where teams still depend on too many manual steps, then make changes testable, progressive and auditable.

The experiences can verify or contradict that statement. That is a useful property.

The reader now knows what to look for: automation, risk reduction, rollout, audit and team autonomy.

## 3. “Responsible for implementing and maintaining…”

A long list follows:

Kubernetes, Terraform, Ansible, GitLab CI, Grafana, Prometheus, ELK, AWS, Azure.

The list proves exposure to tools. It says little about seniority.

Two people can write exactly that sentence after very different assignments.

One implemented an architecture designed elsewhere. The other inherited a failing platform, compared models, got a direction accepted, organized rollback and remained accountable for the result over six months.

Seniority appears in constraints, decisions and consequences.

I would rebuild the experience around four elements:

1. **Situation:** what was blocked or risky;
2. **Scope:** team, production, volume, criticality or duration;
3. **Decision:** the trade-off the person actually owned;
4. **Outcome:** what changed and how the team knows.

For example:

> Took over a Kubernetes platform used by 18 teams after several manual rollbacks. Introduced progressive delivery, stopping criteria and shared runbooks. Recovery time fell from several hours to under 30 minutes for the covered incidents.

If the number is not available, do not invent it.

Precision can come from another form:

> Application teams could return to the previous version without platform-team intervention.

An observable consequence is more useful than a decorative percentage.

## 4. “Supported teams through their digital transformation”

This phrase absorbs everything.

It can hide workshops, a cloud migration, coaching, a new organization, architecture choices or an entire year spent reminding people that the red pipeline might deserve an investigation.

It hides boundaries too.

How many teams? With which mandate? Which disagreement? Which decision actually belonged to the person? What remained with the client?

I would replace the label with the work scene:

> Supported four product teams moving to a shared platform. I clarified responsibilities between developers and the platform team, prepared the first migration paths and facilitated architecture reviews. Product teams kept application choices; the platform took ownership of deployment, identity and observability standards.

The reader can see the level of influence without turning the person into an all-powerful hero.

They can also see the role boundary, which increases credibility.

## A senior profile should reduce uncertainty

The client or recruiter is not buying a complete list of everything the person knows.

They are trying to reduce several uncertainties:

- have they already faced a similar situation?
- do they understand constraints before proposing a tool?
- can they own a trade-off?
- can they work with the affected teams?
- will the system be more operable after they leave?

The profile should provide enough evidence for the conversation to begin at the right level.

That does not require more text. It often requires removing:

- technologies repeated in every assignment;
- qualities nobody can verify;
- titles covering six jobs;
- responsibilities without context;
- results without a method;
- paragraphs describing the company before the work.

The recovered space goes to decisions, constraints and consequences.

## Keep keywords without writing for a robot

Recruitment platforms and LinkedIn searches use exact terms. Removing `Kubernetes`, `SRE`, `Terraform` or `OpenTelemetry` in the name of elegant writing would be counterproductive.

I would distribute them in three places:

1. the title, for the primary role;
2. skills, for technical coverage;
3. experiences, when the tool actually supported a decision.

That structure helps both search engine and human reader.

The engine finds the term. The reader understands why it matters.

## The thirty-second test

After a first read, another person should be able to complete this sentence:

> I would call this person when…

If the answer remains “when I need someone senior in tech,” the positioning still needs work.

A useful answer sounds more like:

> I would call this person when several teams deploy on a platform that has become fragile and we need to standardize it without blocking production.

The profile is not locked into one assignment. It provides an entry point clear enough to be remembered, found and recommended.

Skills can then widen the conversation.
